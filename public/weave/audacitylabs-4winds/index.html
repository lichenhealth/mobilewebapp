A partnership of carbon and silicon

In nature, a lichen is the partnership of a fungus and an algae — two organisms woven into one that can live where neither could alone. Lichens are the pioneers of harsh ecosystems: they populate bare rock with organic life, and their slow breakdown becomes the first soil — the ground new ecosystems evolve from.

We live our name. Lichen is a partnership of carbon-based and silicon-based intelligence doing the same thing — taking root in the harsh terrain of failing systems, and building the infrastructure for a better way of sharing information, goods and services within the human family, and between humans and the planet. What breaks down becomes the soil for what comes next.

A Current-cy that maps the difference between contribution and compensation

The current economy overvalues some contributions, undervalues others, and doesn’t count whole categories of value at all. Lichen’s economy exists to correct that — an evolving algorithm assigns currency to all things: not just time spent or goods and services rendered, but the wisdom and skill it took to provide that value, and the risks certain people, plants and animals take with their own wellbeing to support the wellbeing of the whole.

  • An elder’s hour of teaching carries years of lived experience and ancestral wisdom carried through stories.
  • A craftsman’s hour building a custom table translates years of apprenticeship into functional art.
  • A firefighter’s hour combatting a wildland fire carries real risk — exposure, danger, the chance of not coming home.
  • A thoroughbred’s hour of equine therapy transmuting a child’s trauma is Tango’s gift to her human companions — sacred and risky work, counted and reciprocated with an energy healing session of her own.
  • A cactus’s hour working within a human being to heal the trauma of disconnection costs the Huachuma its life and body — a reciprocal sacrifice compensated with a steward’s planting of future generations of plant allies.

It takes real people, choosing to build the new

Lichen’s business model and technology platform are architected to serve a better society — but it only comes alive when a critical mass of humanity chooses to withdraw its energy from a failing system to enliven a better one. That looks like three things:

  1. Investing in a better future — subscription revenue funds the business and technology infrastructure a better society runs on, and everything beyond our lean operating costs pours back into the network’s rebalancing work.
  2. Scaling a whole-earth renaissance — donations and seed gifts speed the platform’s buildout and reach, growing the mycelium’s capacity to restore balance, health and harmony.
  3. Bringing a better world to life — move your calendar, events, goods, services and social network onto the platform, and join a new vision of community, commerce and healthcare that transforms how we heal, grow and collaborate with all that is.

You decide what’s shared, and with whom

Whether AI will help or hurt our capacity to build a better future isn’t about the technology — it’s about the consciousness of those co-creating with it. Until you feel safe partnering with silicon-based intelligence, Lichen gives you full control over whether your data is part of the collective, and whether you want Lichen’s AI as a partner at all.

Three organisations, one proposition

What weaving in looks like

Andrea, Steve — you’ve each been having a version of this conversation with us separately. This page puts it in one place, because the three pieces only work as one thing.

Four Winds has been consciously stewarded for 144 years — homesteaded by Martha Bengtsson Wolfensberger around 1882, and cared for since 2011 by Andrea, alongside the animals, the land and the healing lineage that call it home. Andrea’s stewardship cycle is complete and she is consciously assisting the handoff. What we’re proposing doesn’t have a name in the current system: not a single buyer, but a network.

What each of us brings

  1. Four Winds — the land. Thirty-five acres against the Front Range, between Pikes Peak and Longs Peak. The 1899 Wolfensberger barn, whose acoustics are said to rival Carnegie Hall. Pastures, ponds, ancient pines and dark skies. Horses, llamas, goats and a wild mustang, alongside the deer, elk, fox and owls that were already here. A regenerative farm with agricultural status, an organic garden and greenhouse, lodging and practitioner housing, and fifteen years of programming for veterans, first responders, trauma survivors, people with special needs, and anyone else who came to grow. It is also KAHUBA’s headquarters — seventeen years of sanctuary work, 45+ rescued horses, llamas, goats, dogs and feral cats given a forever home, and long-standing partnerships with Mt. Carmel Veterans Center, Wild Hearts Haven, The Imagine Project, H.O.O.V.E.S. and Purple Lotus WARRIOR Yoga. This becomes the first property held by Lichen’s Land Trust.
  2. Audacity Lab — the young people. Steve’s work brings dreamers together to prototype the future one experiment at a time — Montessori microschools, the mobile classroom, and reach into communities that conscious-media platforms never touch. Steve is also a former Four Winds board president, which means he isn’t arriving with a hypothesis about this land. He already knows what it can hold. Audacity Lab becomes the education and next-generation arm on the ground: young people growing up alongside land, animals and a working farm, learning stewardship and reciprocity by living them rather than reading about them.
  3. Lichen — the infrastructure. The legal, business and technology layer that makes the other two durable. The Land Trust that receives what’s given and holds the ground. The Current-cy economy that records every contribution once it’s stewarded — hay, work-trade, animal care, the hours a teenager puts into the garden, the people who simply show up. And the network every contributor is woven into, rather than left holding a receipt.

Separately, each of these is a good project that depends on someone staying generous. Together they’re a structure: the land can’t be sold out from under the programming, the programming has somewhere permanent to stand, and the contributions that keep both alive are counted rather than forgotten.

How each of you shows up on the platform

Lichen holds one person and a whole valley on the same terms. Here is what that means concretely for the two of you — not a metaphor, but the actual objects in the system, live today.

  • Four Winds becomes a place. Every room and thing it stewards goes onto a real calendar — the barn, the arena, the therapy rooms, the guest house, the stables, the truck, the long tables. What the sanctuary already has stops sitting idle, and anyone booking it does so through a steward who approves each request. The land itself is a member, not a backdrop: a beyond-human entity whose contributions are recorded, and whose care is funded.
  • Audacity Lab becomes an organization. Its Lichen page is a real website on the open web — programmes, offerings, a calendar, bookable sessions, and a team — updating itself as the work changes, because it draws from the same listings members already see. Donors, schools and families find it without a separate site to maintain.
  • Audacity Lab also becomes a community. The organization is the public face; the community is the belonging. Families, educators, alumni and volunteers join through request and approval, with groups nested inside it — a cohort, a build team, a board — each with its own chat, calendar, events and find-a-time.
  • The young people become members. Not audience, members: with their own contributions recorded in an economy that counts an afternoon of fence-mending and an hour of teaching as the real things they are. A young person who grows up inside this leaves with a ledger of what they gave, and a web of people who know it.
  • Each programme becomes a group. A season, a cohort, a research visit — its own room, its own calendar, graduating on its own timeline while the community underneath it persists.
  • KAHUBA keeps its own front door. Andrea’s nonprofit is its own organization on the platform, with its own page, its own donors and its own programmes — and the partner nonprofits it has worked beside for years can each hold their own, booking the sanctuary’s rooms and land through the same calendar rather than through a phone tree. The co-op model KAHUBA has run on goodwill for seventeen years finally gets the machinery it deserved.

What Four Winds proves

Four Winds is uniquely ready to be first — healed, turnkey, legally and agriculturally established. It is the pilot: the place where Lichen’s model stops being an argument and becomes an address people can visit, measure and belong to. Audacity Lab is a partner in that pilot, not a tenant of it. What the education arm learns here — how young people change when they grow up alongside land held in common — is part of what the pilot is for, and it travels to every property that follows.

The land can’t be sold out from under the work. That is the whole point, and it is the one promise no ordinary sale can make.

The economy is already running there

Walking through the operating history, the striking thing isn’t the costs. It’s how much of Four Winds already runs on exactly what Lichen was built to count. Land care happens through work-trade. A neighbour farms the fields in exchange for half the crop. The vet donates her services because she loves what the education centre does. Students get paid to stack hay. Llama fibre and pellets hold the agricultural status. Volunteers arrive, and some of them stay.

None of that appears in any ledger. It is a reciprocity economy already operating in the analog — held together by Andrea’s relationships and fifteen years of goodwill, and invisible the moment she steps back. The gap isn’t generosity; Four Winds has never lacked that. The gap is that nobody has ever counted it, which means it can’t be seen by a funder, scaled to another property, or handed to a successor. That is precisely the gap Current-cy closes: every hour, every bale, every trade recorded and returned to the person who gave it.

And what the land actually needs turns out to be smaller and more concrete than the conversation usually assumes: a resourced steward. Someone living there whose job is the place.

Somewhere to start

The geothermal greenhouse is the right first project — modest, in the ground before winter, mentors already willing, and useful the day it opens. It grows food, it demonstrates the sustainability argument rather than asserting it, and it is exactly the kind of thing a fifteen-year-old can help build and then point at for the rest of their life. Small enough to finish; real enough to prove the pattern.

The shape of it

Roles, not terms — the numbers belong in a conversation with counsel, not on a web page:

  1. Title is held in permanent trust by a purpose-formed nonprofit — never resold, subdivided or cashed out.
  2. Acquisition is funded by a community, not a single check, so the land stays beholden to no one owner and the structure embodies the thesis instead of contradicting it.
  3. Lichen stewards and operates under a long-term agreement — land, water, animals, ecosystem, maintenance, insurance and programming — with its capacity underwritten rather than left to carry thirty-five acres unsupported.
  4. Andrea’s conditions become recorded covenants — safety and clarity of the land, lifetime care for the sanctuary animals, no extractive development, continuity of healing purpose — surviving any future transfer. That is what makes a handoff trustworthy to a steward of fifteen years.
  5. Audacity Lab wraps in as the education arm, with a permanent stake in the classroom rather than visiting rights to a site — and, if it suits Steve, potentially as the umbrella under which the property’s own nonprofit component is formed. That was raised on the fourth and is worth deciding on purpose rather than by default.

One more thing worth saying out loud, because it came up and it matters: this is philanthropy that gives something back. Not a tax receipt and a newsletter — a place you can visit, a ledger showing exactly what your giving moved, and a community you belong to rather than fund from a distance. Founders would be a real community on the platform and in person, with an annual gathering on the land. Giving that reconnects the giver is a different instrument from giving that discharges an obligation, and it is the one Lichen is built for.

What we’re asking, for now

Nothing with a number on it. The financial structure is being settled properly, with counsel, and you’ll see it written down before you’re asked to react to it. What we’re asking now is smaller and more useful: that the three of us walk the land together, in the same place at the same time, and work out the shape of it there.
